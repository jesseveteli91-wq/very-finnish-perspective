Pekka has paid extra for a quiet seat. He now has to open a sandwich. He lifts one corner of the wrapper. Three rows away, a man takes off his headphones. They were already off. He just likes people to know that he heard.

At home this morning, the paper had made absolutely no sound. There had also been a dishwasher running, a radio on, and a dog next door explaining something to a van. In that kitchen you could have wrapped a bicycle in sheet metal and described the result as fairly quiet. Pekka made a cheese sandwich, cut it on the diagonal, and sealed the paper with a little sticker shaped like a tomato. His wife had drawn a smile on the tomato. At the time this seemed kind.

The train has been moving for forty minutes. The seat opposite is occupied by a woman reading a very large book. Beside her is a small boy with a pencil. He has drawn a train with eleven wheels along one side. Pekka would normally have several questions about the suspension. Today he is glad the boy has given himself something to do.

On the window there is a little picture of a phone with a line through it. Under that, a picture of a person with a finger to their lips. No picture of a sandwich. Pekka checks the other window. Same two people, same policy gap. He puts the package on his knee. A knee has a little give. A folding table is basically a public address system for lunch.

He tries a different corner. If you have ever tried to open a packet slowly, you will know that this doesn't actually make the sound smaller. It just gives everybody time to find where it's coming from. The woman across the aisle keeps her eyes on her book. Her finger, however, has been holding the same line since the first corner. Pekka gently folds the corner back. It makes the same noise going home.

There is an obvious solution. Trains make announcements. All Pekka has to do is wait for someone to say the name of a town, and use that town to take the top off his lunch. He puts his thumb under the tomato. There is a little electronic chime. He pulls. The chime has two notes. He remembered three.

The voice comes on a second later. Welcome aboard, says the voice. Pekka could open the whole thing now. Unfortunately the welcome is in Finnish and he is listening in case there is anything important. Then it comes in Swedish. He listens to see how much Swedish he remembers. Then it comes in English. He knows the information now, in two languages, but he would quite like to know whether he got the Swedish right. The announcement ends. He is still holding the tomato. Somewhere behind him a man has managed to eat an entire apple.

A tunnel appears ahead. This is promising. As a child Pekka remembers trains making a tremendous noise in tunnels. He leans towards the glass to estimate how much tunnel there is. The train goes in. Everything outside goes black. The only change inside is that the window now contains a very clear reflection of a middle-aged man looking furtively at his own trousers. The boy adds a passenger to his drawing.

Pekka looks down. The little corner he lifted has exposed perhaps two millimetres of bread. He could eat the exposed portion. At this rate, by the time they reach Tampere he will have eaten the outline. The cheese will arrive intact. He slips a finger under the fold and notices the paper is held shut only by the sticker. So it isn't necessary to open the paper. It is necessary to remove a single tomato. This feels much more like something he can put on a list and finish.

He peels it from the left. The smile stretches. He changes hands and peels it from the right. For a moment the tomato has the facial expression of someone being photographed for a staff badge. The sticker comes free without a sound. Pekka sets it on the back of his hand. The paper stays exactly where it is. He has removed the lock from a door that says pull.

Then the woman turns a page. It's a lovely broad, dry noise. During that noise, he could have unfolded a corner. He looks at the size of the book. There must be a hundred and fifty opportunities left. He prepares his hand, and waits. She reads the left page. She reads the right page. Her hand moves towards the top corner. Pekka begins to lift. She puts in a bookmark and closes the book.

The boy looks at the sandwich, then at his sheet of paper. Without speaking he draws a little square beside the train. Then two diagonal lines. An arrow. It's a diagram of how to open a sandwich. Pekka studies it carefully. It's quite a good diagram. Up to this point in his life he has attended three workplace training courses which were less directly applicable to his circumstances.

A shadow crosses the paper. The ticket inspector is standing beside him. Pekka has been holding his ticket ready on his phone since before the previous station. He presents the screen. The inspector scans it. The machine makes a beep so magnificent that a person in the next carriage could probably tell which seat he has. The inspector moves on. The boy quietly adds a very large exclamation mark to the diagram.

If you have also missed the perfect moment because you were too busy appreciating how perfect it was, you may subscribe. Take your time. The button is considerably quieter than this sandwich.

Pekka finally stands. Between the two carriages there is a little space where people can make calls. He has seen a man go in there to discuss a garden shed for twenty minutes. Surely he can take in a sandwich for six seconds. He carries the parcel level in both hands. The automatic door opens. The door closes. Behind glass, in full view of the entire carriage, he opens the paper like a man starting the first fire of winter.

Nothing. Barely a whisper. He looks at the wrapper. He looks back through the door at the people sitting in the warm, quiet compartment. Out here the wheels are loud, the ventilation is on, and a man on the phone is explaining that the shed must have a window. This, acoustically speaking, is his kitchen. He takes one extremely satisfactory bite.

When he returns to his seat, the woman has opened her bag. Out comes a sandwich, in precisely the same paper. The man with the headphones has a packet of biscuits balanced on one knee. Three rows away somebody is holding a banana halfway through the act of separating it from another banana. Apparently what the quiet carriage needed was a lunch break. What it got was a demonstration of where to have one.

One by one they head for the little space between the carriages. The man on the phone says he will call back. The boy adds a twelfth wheel. Pekka smooths out his wrapper, folds it in half, and writes tomorrow's shopping list on the clean side. Cheese. Bread. One perfectly ordinary box with a lid.

The boy taps the back of Pekka's hand. He wants the little tomato for his drawing. Pekka peels it off and places it in the driver's window. The train finally has a driver. The driver looks very happy. At the next station Pekka steps onto the platform with the empty paper still in his pocket. His phone lights up. A message from his wife. Did you find the crisps underneath?
