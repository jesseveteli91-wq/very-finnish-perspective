This Finnish man never eats breakfast. Today, he has set an alarm for six forty-five. Breakfast is included in the room price. He intends to be included in the breakfast. Yesterday he paid for one night in a hotel. Overnight, this has become a personal agreement with a croissant. His name is Pekka. He has packed walking shoes, a phone charger, and a very quiet determination to receive everything mentioned in the booking confirmation.

At home, breakfast is coffee consumed while standing next to a sink. Sometimes he opens the fridge, looks inside, and closes it. That is the fruit course. But at a hotel there are little bowls. There are lids you can lift. There is somebody whose actual job is replacing sausages. Pekka has not travelled three hundred kilometres to disrespect this infrastructure. He gets out of bed before the first alarm has finished its sentence.

His wife asks if he is hungry. Pekka says, a little. This is not true. He is financially awake. She reminds him that they are on holiday. Exactly, he says. We have time to do this properly. He checks the breakfast hours again. Seven until ten. Three hours. At work, a three-hour meeting would be a serious concern. Here, with the correct arrangement of cheese, it looks like a reasonable use of the morning.

At six fifty-eight, he is outside the restaurant. Not waiting. Just standing in a place where opening will happen. Another man is there already. They exchange one nod. Neither mentions the time. Mentioning it would turn a coincidence into a club. Through the glass door, a member of staff adjusts a serving spoon. Both men watch with the calm intensity usually reserved for reversing a trailer into a narrow garage.

The door opens. The other man makes a small gesture meaning you first. Pekka makes a smaller gesture meaning no, you first. They have both arrived early to make the most of their morning. They spend the first part of it trying to give it away. Finally, a woman with two children walks between them. This solves the problem. They follow her in, quietly grateful for decisive leadership.

Pekka does not choose the nearest table. First he checks the distance to the coffee, the distance to the buffet, and whether anyone could sit directly behind him. A window is nice, but a window cannot refill your cup. He selects a table with a clear view of the food and one side protected by a wall. It is a small table for two. In his head it already has a supply route.

He puts his jacket over the chair. There. The table is occupied. Not by a person, but by a jacket with responsibilities. His wife puts down her bag. Now there are two witnesses. Pekka checks that the sleeve is visible from the buffet. He has seen people lose a good table through inadequate jacket placement. Well, he has imagined it. For him, this is usually enough to justify taking precautions.

His wife picks up a plate. Pekka does not. You cannot begin with a plate. A plate commits you to decisions before the information is complete. He walks along the buffet empty-handed. Bread. Eggs. Bacon. Mushrooms. Tomatoes. Some kind of seed. The seed is noted, but not considered urgent. He reaches the far end and discovers the pancakes. Good thing he looked. The entire plan has just changed.

He returns to the beginning by a different route, to avoid looking as though he is inspecting the buffet. Unfortunately, this brings him past the buffet again. He now knows where everything is, but three other people have learned where he is. One of them asks if he is in the queue. No, Pekka says. Just looking. This sounds exactly like what you say in a shop before walking out without buying anything.

The first plate is sensible. Some eggs, a little bacon, one tomato. Pekka believes in balance. The tomato provides the appearance of it. He leaves a useful gap between the eggs and the bread because a wet slice of bread is a problem nobody should have to solve on holiday. At the next tray he sees a second kind of egg. This was not visible during the inspection. He feels the system could communicate better.

Then he reaches the bacon. There is one piece left in the tray. An ordinary piece. Not especially large. Not especially impressive. A moment ago, he wanted bacon. Now he wants evidence that nobody else wants bacon. He looks to the left. Another guest looks to the right. Both look at the ceiling with a sudden interest in hotel ventilation. The serving tongs remain where they are. Nobody wishes to be remembered as the bacon person.

Pekka takes a mushroom. The other guest takes a mushroom. Neither of them wanted a mushroom. They are now participating in a vegetable-based peace agreement. A member of staff arrives with a full tray of fresh bacon and places it in front of them. Both men take two pieces immediately. There was never a shortage of appetite. Only a shortage of socially acceptable bacon. Pekka returns to his table feeling that the matter was handled well.

The jacket has done its job. His wife is eating yoghurt. She has somehow managed to obtain food without developing a route. Pekka sits down and places his cutlery neatly beside the plate. For a moment, everything is ready. Then he notices that he has no drink. This is the trouble with a buffet. Every successful decision reveals another small thing you have forgotten. He stands up again. The jacket is promoted back into active service.

The coffee machine has seven buttons. Pekka requires one coffee. The machine would like a short discussion about his identity. Espresso. Americano. Cappuccino. Latte. Something with two words and a picture of a cloud. There is also hot water, which feels like a confession. He studies the screen. Behind him, a guest arrives carrying a cup. Pekka can feel the queue forming without turning around. He presses the button with the least milk in the picture.

The machine begins. First it makes a loud grinding noise. Then a quieter grinding noise. Then no noise at all. Pekka does not move the cup. The machine has not explicitly told him their relationship is finished. A few drops appear. He waits. Another drop. He waits again. The person behind him reaches towards the buttons. Pekka lifts his cup at exactly the wrong moment and receives a final drop on the saucer. There. A little coffee for everybody.

He also gets orange juice. At home, he does not drink orange juice. Here, it has arrived in a tall glass dispenser, which makes it seem like an opportunity. The glass is small enough that drinking from it feels like taking medicine. He fills it carefully. Not quite to the top. The walk back includes a turn, and he has considered the consequences. He returns with the concentration of a man transporting an open container of paint.

The bread section needs a separate visit. There are rolls, dark bread, sliced bread, and bread that appears to have been designed for a photograph. Pekka chooses a roll. It is warm. This is a strong development. The butter is cold. This is a complication. He tries to spread it. The roll moves. The butter does not. He has brought a knife to a negotiation in which the other side is a small refrigerated brick.

Nearby, a toaster has a moving belt. Pekka puts a slice in. The slice disappears. He leans slightly forward. There is no window through which to check its progress. Another guest puts in two slices and walks away. Remarkable trust. Pekka stays. His toast comes out pale, so he sends it around again. On the second trip it returns with a completely different personality. He places cheese on it. Cheese has resolved more difficult situations than this.

Back at the table, Pekka has eaten half the warm roll. He puts the other half neatly on the plate and stands up to fetch a little more juice. The whole trip should take twenty seconds. His wife has gone for fruit. For the first time, the table is defended only by the jacket. The jacket has been excellent with other guests. It has no formal training in communicating with restaurant staff.

When Pekka returns, the plate is gone. The knife is gone. The half roll is gone. The table is beautifully clean. He stops with the juice in his hand. A member of staff smiles at him from across the room. He smiles back. He cannot explain that he was still emotionally connected to the bread. Technically, there is more bread ten metres away. But that was his bread. They had already been through the butter together.

His wife comes back and asks why he is standing. Nothing, Pekka says. Plate disappeared. She suggests getting another one. He nods. Of course. A very practical solution. He goes to the buffet and selects a replacement roll. This one is slightly larger. He does not think of it as compensation. He simply believes that after an unexpected setback, you should not reduce the scope of the project.

Now come the pancakes. His wife says she thought he was finished. Finished with the first part, Pekka explains. Pancakes are a different matter. There is jam, honey, and a spoon resting in something he hopes is cream. He chooses jam. He can identify jam without asking questions. One pancake would look cautious. Three would look committed. He takes two, a number that allows him to maintain a modest public position.

Next to the pancakes is a bowl of yoghurt. Beside the yoghurt are little jars of seeds. Earlier he did not consider these urgent. Now, after the bacon, butter, cheese, and pancakes, he feels their moment has arrived. He adds one spoonful to a small bowl. His wife asks what they are. Seeds, he says. Yes, but which seeds? Good ones, he says. The label is facing away from him. He is prepared to trust the hotel.

At last, Pekka puts down his fork. He is full. Properly full. He has achieved the original objective and several things that were not in the original objective. Outside the window, people are walking somewhere. They look light and mobile. His wife suggests a walk. Pekka agrees. In a moment. He is waiting for the breakfast to settle into its final arrangement. There is no point rushing this stage. Some of the bread is still finding a place.

Then he looks at the clock. Nine fifty-eight. Breakfast ends at ten. Suddenly the room feels different. The food is still there, but it now has a deadline. Pekka has been sitting comfortably for ten minutes. He no longer needs anything. Then a member of staff closes the lid on an empty tray. The sound is small. The effect is immediate. Pekka looks towards the pastries. There is one croissant he has not yet had a reason to consider.

He stands. His wife looks up. I thought you were full. I am, Pekka says. He continues walking. These two statements are not connected in the way she expects. This is not hunger. It is the last chance to make a decision. In thirty seconds, choosing nothing will stop being a choice. He takes the croissant. It is slightly smaller than he expected. Good. He had been worried about being unreasonable.

He sits down with it and takes a small bite. It is very nice. This is inconvenient, because now he has to admit the final visit was worthwhile. His wife asks whether he wants to do this again tomorrow. Pekka pauses. They are staying two nights. He had temporarily forgotten this part of the booking. He looks at the croissant, then at the restaurant door. Tomorrow, he says, maybe we can come a little later.

Back in the room, his wife puts on her walking shoes. Pekka sits on the edge of the bed and changes the alarm. Six fifty. Five extra minutes. It is a holiday, after all. Before putting the phone down, he checks the booking confirmation once more. Breakfast included. Both mornings. Good. Nothing has changed. If you recognise this man, subscribe to Very Finnish Perspective. We will see you next time. Pekka will see you at breakfast. Slightly before it opens.
